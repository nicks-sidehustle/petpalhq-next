whenNotToBuy: |
  Skip GPS containment entirely if your property is small. PetSafe publishes its no-subscription Guardian for properties of three-quarters of an acre or larger, and that floor reflects a physical limit rather than a marketing choice — satellite positioning is not precise enough to hold a tight boundary reliably. On a townhouse lot, physical fencing or a leash is the honest answer.

  Skip it if your dog is below the published weight floors. The PetSafe Guardian is designed for dogs over 25 pounds; SpotOn publishes a floor of 15 pounds with a 10-to-26-inch neck; Halo publishes over 10 pounds with an 8-to-30-inch neck. Fitting a collar outside its published range is a safety decision, not a sizing preference.

  Skip static-correction containment if the welfare position matters to you, and read it before deciding rather than after. The AAHA's 2015 Canine and Feline Behavior Management Guidelines list electronic shock collars among aversive techniques and state that none of those tools and methods should be used to either teach or alter behavior. Almost every bad outcome in this category traces back to one confusion, and the industry's own vocabulary causes it. A "virtual fence" sold by a tracker company is a phone notification. A "virtual fence" sold by a containment company is a boundary the collar tries to enforce. Same two words, entirely different product, and a household that buys the first believing it bought the second finds out on the day the gate is left open.

This hub separates the two categories, prices the subscriptions from the manufacturers' own live pages, and states the veterinary-body position on static correction in full — because that position exists, it is not ambiguous, and a guide that hid it would be selling rather than advising.

## Two Categories, Two Different Jobs

Everything on PetPalHQ in this space sorts into one of two buckets.

- **Containment** tries to prevent the animal leaving a boundary. It is a collar that delivers a warning and then a static correction as the dog approaches the line. SpotOn, Halo, and the PetSafe Guardian are all containment.
- **Recovery** tells you where the animal is once it has already gone. It is a cellular GPS tag with an app behind it. Tractive and Fi are recovery.

Nothing sold to consumers does both jobs equally well from a single device except the Halo, and it charges a permanent subscription for the privilege. Most households that care seriously about escape end up owning one of each — a fence for the boundary and a tag for the day the boundary fails.

## What a GPS Fence Can and Cannot Do

The capabilities are real. So are the limits, and the limits are published by the manufacturers themselves rather than uncovered by critics.

| The claim | What the manufacturer actually publishes |
|---|---|
| "Works on any property" | PetSafe publishes the no-subscription Guardian for properties **three-quarters of an acre or larger** |
| "Fits any dog" | Guardian: **over 25 pounds**, 13–28 in neck. SpotOn: **at least 15 pounds**, 10–26 in. Halo: **over 10 pounds**, 8–30 in |
| "Keeps your dog safe in the yard" | VCA: invisible fences "do not prevent hazards from entering the yard" — stray dogs and wildlife still get in |
| "Your dog can't get out" | VCA: a frightened or highly motivated dog "may breach the electric fence, ignoring the electric stimulus" |

Those last two rows are the ones that change decisions. A virtual fence is a one-directional deterrent addressed to your dog. It has no effect at all on what comes into the yard, and it is a deterrent rather than a wall for the dog it is addressed to.

## The Correction Question, Stated in Full

Every containment product on this page works by delivering a static correction. The professional bodies have published positions on that class of tool, and they are not neutral.

The American Animal Hospital Association's 2015 Canine and Feline Behavior Management Guidelines list electronic shock collars among aversive techniques and state that "none of those tools and methods should be used to either teach or alter behavior." The same guidelines warn that animals "repeatedly exposed to inescapable unpleasant or painful stimuli may develop learned helplessness."

The American Veterinary Society of Animal Behavior's 2021 Position Statement on Humane Dog Training recommends "that only reward-based training methods are used for all dog training," and says an appropriate trainer "should avoid any use of training tools that involve pain (choke chains, prong collars, or electronic shock collars)." The RSPCA's guidance is that "the electric shock can cause physical problems. It can also cause behaviour issues linked with pain, fear and stress."

Two honest observations about applying that to containment specifically. First, none of those statements is about GPS fences as such — AVSAB's is about training methods, and it does not mention containment at all. Second, the tool class is the same, and a reader deciding whether to put a correction collar on their dog deserves to know the professional position before they buy rather than after.

The practical consequence for this hub is simple. If the welfare argument is decisive for you, the recovery tier is a complete product category with none of it attached, and it starts at $49.99.

## What a Tracker Can and Cannot Do

Tractive's Virtual Fences page states the mechanism in one line: "If your pet crosses a safe zone boundary or enters a no-go zone, you'll receive an instant alert to your phone." Nothing on that page describes a correction, a stimulus, or any containment mechanism, because there is not one.

That is a genuine capability and a real limit at the same time. An alert on your phone is enormously valuable if you are home and the dog just left. It is worth nothing at all if the phone is in another room, and it prevents nothing in either case.

Trackers also carry a hard dependency: the device holds a SIM, so it needs both an active plan and mobile coverage wherever the animal actually went. Tractive's published plans run from $9 a month on a one-year Basic plan through to $5 a month on a five-year Premium plan.

## The Subscription Fork Runs Through Both Categories

This is where the real money lives, and where the published rates diverge most sharply. All figures below were re-fetched from the manufacturers' own pages on August 21, 2026.

- **SpotOn** — the fence needs no plan at all. Tracking is the paid add-on: $9.95 monthly, $8.49 a month billed annually at $101.88, or $7.49 a month on a two-year plan at $179.76.
- **Halo** — the fence itself requires a plan. Published tiers start at $9.99 a month for Bronze, $14.99 for Silver, and $19.99 for Gold, with additional collars at $9.99 a month and a plan required for each collar. Annual totals are not published as dollar figures.
- **PetSafe Guardian** — "No subscription fees or hidden costs – ever."
- **PetSafe Guardian GPS 2.0** — the tracking-equipped sibling does charge: "$9.99 for 1 month (includes a 1-month trial) or $99 for a 1-year plan."
- **Tractive** — Basic at $9 a month on a one-year plan ($108) or $6 on a two-year ($144); Premium at $10, $7, and $5 a month on one-, two-, and five-year plans ($120, $168, $300).

One structural note that the sticker prices hide: Halo charges per collar. A two-dog household on Bronze pays twice, every month, for as long as both dogs wear a collar. Over a ten-year ownership window that is the difference between the cheapest collar on the page and the most expensive system on it.

## Cats Have Trackers, Not Containment

There is no cat equivalent of a GPS fence, and there should not be. Every cat product in this space is a recovery device.

The buying constraints are consequently different. Fit is by weight, and it is published per variant rather than per brand — Tractive sells several cat models with different floors, and the one linked on this page states "for Cats 6.5 lbs+" in its own listing title. Read the floor on the exact variant you are buying. A breakaway collar is non-negotiable for any outdoor cat, which means accepting that the collar and the tracker on it can be lost together. That is a feature: a cat hung on a fence by a non-breakaway collar is a far worse outcome than a lost tracker.

## Training Comes Before Unsupervised Use

None of these systems is a set-and-forget purchase, and the manufacturers themselves build introduction programmes into their onboarding rather than shipping a collar you switch on.

The reason is visible in VCA's account of how these systems fail: a dog that is frightened or highly motivated may breach the boundary and ignore the stimulus entirely. A dog that has been taught where the edge is, using flags and supervised sessions and reward-based reinforcement, is a dog that retreats from the line rather than testing it. A dog that has only been corrected at it has learned that the yard perimeter is where unpleasant things happen — which is the mechanism behind the learned-helplessness warning in the AAHA's guidelines.

If you take one thing from this hub into a purchase, take this: the collar is the second thing you buy. The first is the time to introduce it properly, and if that time does not exist in your household, neither does the containment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is the actual difference between a GPS fence and a GPS tracker?**

A: A GPS fence attempts to keep the dog inside a boundary, using a warning and then a static correction as the dog approaches the line. A GPS tracker reports the animal's location and can send an alert when it leaves an area. Tractive's own Virtual Fences page says that when the pet crosses a safe-zone boundary "you'll receive an instant alert to your phone" — no correction and no containment is involved. If your goal is prevention, you need containment; if your goal is finding the animal, you need a tracker.

**Q: Do any GPS fences work without a subscription?**

A: Yes, two on this page. SpotOn states that without a plan you can still create GPS fences and train your dog to stay in — what a plan adds is location visibility and tracking. PetSafe's Guardian product page states "No subscription fees or hidden costs – ever." The Halo Collar 5 is the opposite case: Halo's own home page says a Pack Membership Plan is required to create, edit, and use wireless dog fences, and a plan is required for each collar.

**Q: How small a yard can a GPS fence handle?**

A: PetSafe publishes its no-subscription Guardian for properties of three-quarters of an acre or larger, and that floor is a physical constraint rather than a marketing one — satellite positioning is not precise enough to hold a tight boundary consistently. On a small suburban lot, physical fencing is the answer, and a tracker covers the escape-recovery half.

**Q: What do veterinary organisations say about static-correction collars?**

A: They are not neutral on them. The AAHA's 2015 Canine and Feline Behavior Management Guidelines list electronic shock collars among aversive techniques and state that none of those tools and methods should be used to teach or alter behavior. AVSAB's 2021 position statement recommends reward-based training methods only and says trainers should avoid tools that involve pain, naming electronic shock collars specifically. The RSPCA says the electric shock can cause physical problems and behaviour issues linked with pain, fear and stress. None of those statements is specifically about GPS containment, but the tool class is the same and the position deserves to be read before a purchase rather than after.

**Q: Will a virtual fence keep other animals out of my yard?**

A: No. VCA Animal Hospitals states the point directly: invisible fences prevent dogs from leaving the yard but do not prevent hazards from entering it, and stray dogs and wild animals can still access the yard and interact aggressively with the resident dog. If keeping things out is part of what you need, only physical fencing does that.

**Q: Is there a GPS containment system for cats?**

A: No. Every GPS product for cats is a recovery device — live location and, on some models, activity and wellness data. That is the correct state of the category. If your cat goes outdoors, the purchase is a tracker on a breakaway collar, sized to the published fit range for the model you buy.
