Chinchillas and rats end up in the same guide for a reason that has nothing to do with them being similar animals. They are not. But they are the two species most often housed in the same product — the tall, powder-coated, multi-level wire cage — and they are the two the rest of the small-mammal shelf quietly skips. PetPalHQ has dedicated cage guides for ferrets, hamsters, guinea pigs and sugar gliders. This one closes the gap.

They also share the specification that decides the purchase, and it is not the one the listings advertise.

## Floor plan, not height

Every cage in this category sells on height. Fifty-five inches. Fifty-nine inches. Six tiers. What the listings do not do is measure themselves against a published veterinary minimum, so here is that comparison, because it splits this shortlist in half.

**VCA Animal Hospitals states the minimum enclosure for a chinchilla as 3 feet by 2 feet by 3 feet for a single animal, and 3 feet by 2 feet by 5 feet for a pair.** That is a floor area of 36 by 24 inches, in both cases.

| Cage | Footprint | Meets the 36 x 24 in floor area? |
|---|---|---|
| Critter Nation Double | 36 x 24 in | Yes — and 63 in tall, clearing the 5-foot pair minimum |
| Critter Nation Single | 36 x 25 in | Yes — and 38.5 in tall, clearing the 3-foot single minimum |
| HABUTWAY 55" | 25 x 18 in | No — 30% shorter and 25% narrower than the minimum |
| VIWAT 6-Tier | 32 x 21 in | No — under on both axes |

Two of these cages name chinchillas in their listings and do not meet the published minimum floor area for one. They are not bad cages — they are **rat cages**, and for rats they are reasonable. But height does not substitute for floor plan, and no amount of vertical space makes an 18-inch-deep cage into a chinchilla enclosure.

Note also *how* VCA's pair minimum grows: from 3x2x3 to 3x2x5 feet. The footprint stays identical and the **height** increases by two feet. Adding a second chinchilla means adding vertical volume, which is precisely what the Critter Nation Double's 63 inches delivers and its 38.5-inch Single does not.

## Bar spacing: the number that keeps a rat inside

For rats, the governing spec is different and it is unusually clear-cut. **The American Fancy Rat and Mouse Association states that rats should be housed in cages with ½ inch spacing between the bars** — and adds the warning that makes the figure load-bearing: *"Even an adult rat can still get its head stuck in ¾ inch spacing, so they are really not safe."*

Every pick in this guide publishes a spacing figure at or below that standard: half an inch on both Critter Nations and the HABUTWAY, 0.4 inches on the VIWAT. That was a requirement for inclusion here rather than a happy coincidence — a large multi-level cage that does not publish its bar spacing is not one we can recommend for a rat, because the single most important safety number is missing. At least one otherwise well-priced cage in this class is absent from this guide for exactly that reason.

For chinchillas the limits are looser and come from the **Merck Veterinary Manual**, which sets mesh openings at no wider than 1 by 2 inches on the sides of the cage and ½ by ½ inch on the bottom. All four cages here sit well inside the side limit.

One thing the tight-spacing marketing invites you to conclude, which the authority does not support: **narrower bars are not the answer for babies.** AFRMA's guidance is that *wire cages with levels should not be used for moms and young litters* at all, because babies can be dragged up shelves, fall, or squeeze through. If you are breeding or fostering, the answer is a different cage type, not a smaller gap.

## The floor and the ramps

This is where cheap cages in this category cut corners, and where the veterinary guidance is least ambiguous.

**Merck on rats: "The cage floor should be solid; wire mesh or grid flooring can damage rats' feet and legs."** Elsewhere it explains the mechanism — rats and mice can trap their feet, especially hindlimbs, in the openings, resulting in fractures and injuries. Merck and AFRMA independently give the same figure for any wire shelf or ramp the animal must walk on: openings no wider than ½ by ½ inch, because a rat's back foot can catch in 1-by-½-inch wire and break a leg.

The RSPCA quantifies how strongly rats feel about this, and the number is remarkable: **rats will lift doors weighing up to 83% of their own body weight in order to leave a cage with a grid floor and enter one with a solid floor.**

**For chinchillas, VCA prescribes covering at least part of a wire cage floor** with Plexiglass, non-treated wood or other easily-cleaned solid flooring, to prevent pododermatitis — sore hocks. Merck adds the sharper warning: *tibial fractures commonly occur in young chinchillas that catch a hindleg in wide floor-mesh grating.*

The practical consequence for shopping: check what solid surface ships **in the box**. The Critter Nation cages arrive with removable plastic pans, plastic-insert shelves, and reversible washable Happy Feet covers for their three wire ramps — the ramps themselves are bare wire, and the covers are what make them safe. The HABUTWAY ships a removable flannel ramp sleeve for the same purpose. Whatever you buy, the wire the animal stands on needs covering, and spare covers are a sensible first accessory purchase.

## Height, falls, and rearing up

Two RSPCA figures turn "multi-level" from a marketing word into something you can actually check.

**For chinchillas: "Make sure there are no uninterrupted heights greater than 60cm to stop them from hurting themselves."** That is about 23.6 inches of maximum clear drop. A 63-inch cage complies only if you stagger the shelves so no open vertical run exceeds that — which is exactly what the Critter Nation's height-adjustable shelves are for. Buying tall and then leaving the middle empty is the failure mode.

**For rats: "this will require an area with a height of around 18 cm for juveniles up to 150g, 22 cm for rats up to 450g and 30 cm for large, adult males"** — the headroom needed to rear up on the hind legs, which rats do constantly when exploring. Thirty centimetres is about 11.8 inches.

Put those together and a six-tier cage stops looking like six times the value. Dividing a published 59-inch height across six platforms leaves roughly ten inches per level before platform thickness — under the rear-up figure for a large male. **Fewer, taller levels beat more, shorter ones.** If a cage arrives with more tiers than headroom, take some out.

## Ventilation, and why it is a lifespan question for rats

Rats are prone to chronic respiratory infections, and the housing decision genuinely moves the outcome. Merck is explicit that **aquariums are not suitable cages for rats and mice, because air circulation is inadequate and therefore ammonia builds up.** VCA calls poor environmental ventilation *a major cause of respiratory disease in pet rodents*, allowing ammonia from the urine to build up and irritate the airways. And Merck states the stakes plainly: **rats with chronic respiratory disease rarely live longer than 2 years**, against a normal span of 18 to 36 months.

Two practical follow-ons. Bedding matters: Merck states that cedar, pine and other wood shavings contain oils that can irritate rats' sensitive respiratory tracts and are not recommended, and that recycled paper products marketed as bedding are safe and easy to clean. And cleaning frequency matters more than cleaning intensity — Merck's instruction is to avoid a buildup of ammonia by changing litter often enough. Note MidWest's own restriction here, which sits in slight tension with that: its assembly documents specify mild, non-abrasive cleaners and water only, to protect the finish and the pans. Clean often, gently.

## The chinchilla constraint that outranks the cage

Before any of this matters, the room has to be cool enough. **VCA states the optimal environmental temperature as 55 to 68°F (10 to 20°C), that it is essential to keep household temperatures below 80°F (27°C), and that humidity should stay below 40 to 50 percent** — because chinchillas are very susceptible to heat stroke. Merck gives a slightly wider adapted range of 65 to 80°F, notes that higher temperatures combined with high humidity can cause heatstroke, and offers a rule worth memorising: *add the unit values of the temperature (Fahrenheit) and humidity, and consider any value above 150 to be dangerous.*

Merck also lists the daily requirement no cage includes: **a dust bath, 2 to 4 inches deep, for about ten minutes every day.** Make sure whatever cage you buy has a shelf that will hold that container.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What size cage does a chinchilla actually need?**
A: VCA Animal Hospitals gives a minimum of 3 feet by 2 feet by 3 feet for a single chinchilla and 3 feet by 2 feet by 5 feet for a pair. Note that the footprint is the same in both cases — 36 by 24 inches — and the pair requirement is met by adding height, not floor area. That is why the Critter Nation Double at a listed 36 by 24 by 63 inches is the pair-appropriate cage in this guide and its 38.5-inch Single is not. Also worth knowing: the RSPCA says chinchillas should be kept with at least one companion unless a vet advises otherwise, so the pair figure is the realistic target for most households.

**Q: What bar spacing is safe for pet rats?**
A: Half an inch. AFRMA states that rats should be housed in cages with ½ inch spacing between the bars, and warns that even an adult rat can get its head stuck in ¾ inch spacing, so those cages are really not safe. Both Critter Nation cages and the HABUTWAY publish half an inch; the VIWAT publishes 0.4 inches, which is tighter still. Any wire shelf or ramp the rat walks on should be ½ by ½ inch, per both AFRMA and Merck, because a back foot caught in 1-by-½-inch wire can break a leg.

**Q: Is the Critter Nation really worth double the price of a budget cage?**
A: For chinchillas, the answer is structural rather than a matter of quality: the budget cages here do not meet the published minimum floor area, so they are not the same purchase. For rats, the case is narrower and more honest — the budget cages have adequate bar spacing and cost less. What the Critter Nation adds is the footprint, the swing-up ramp that splits the cage in two for introductions and quarantine, the plastic pans and shelf inserts, and an upgrade path via the Add-On Unit. It is worth noting that not every authority crowns it: Forever Feisty Chinchilla Rescue recommends Martin's Cages first and describes the Critter Nation as "very popular" rather than as its top pick.

**Q: Can I house a chinchilla and a rat together?**
A: No. They are different species with different temperature requirements, different diets, different lifespans and no reason to be introduced. Keep chinchillas with chinchillas and rats with rats — both species need same-species company, and both need it in same-sex groups unless neutered. The RSPCA states rats should never be housed singly and that groups of up to five of the same sex usually work well.

**Q: Do these cages need solid floors, or are wire floors fine?**
A: Rats need solid. Merck is unambiguous that the cage floor should be solid because wire mesh or grid flooring can damage rats' feet and legs, and the RSPCA found rats will lift doors weighing up to 83% of their body weight to get off a grid floor. Chinchillas are the more permissive case — Merck says they are easily housed on either wire-mesh or solid-bottom cages, but adds they need a solid area to rest on so the bottoms of their feet do not get sores, and VCA prescribes covering at least part of a wire floor to prevent pododermatitis. In practice, cover the wire for both.

**Q: How hard is the Critter Nation to put together?**
A: Harder than a flat-pack shelf, and MidWest says so itself rather than leaving it to reviews. Its Model 162 assembly document runs to 21 numbered steps and states that although the cage can be assembled by one person, due to size you may find it easier assembled with two people, and that a rubber mallet is also recommended. MidWest publishes no assembly-time estimate, so any figure you see quoted is a customer's, not the manufacturer's.

**Q: Does the Critter Nation warranty cover chewing damage?**
A: No. The warranty runs one year from original purchase for defects in workmanship or material, and MidWest's own document states it does not cover defects caused by any animal, or physical abuse to or misuse of the cage. For an animal that chews plastic shelf inserts, that exclusion is the relevant one — budget for replacement pans and covers as consumables rather than warranty claims.

**Q: How long will I have this cage?**
A: Longer than you might think, which is the real argument for buying above the minimum. VCA puts chinchilla life expectancy at 15 to 20 years and Merck says up to 20. Rats are the opposite case at 2 to 3 years per VCA, or 18 to 36 months per Merck. So one good cage covers a single chinchilla's entire life, or roughly six to eight consecutive generations of rats. Amortised either way, the gap between the cheapest and the most expensive cage here is small.
