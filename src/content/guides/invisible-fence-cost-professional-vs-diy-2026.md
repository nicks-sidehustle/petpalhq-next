The question behind "how much does an invisible fence cost" is almost never really about the hardware. It is about whether the professionally installed version — the one with a brand name, a dealer, a trainer and a service contract — is worth several times what a box on a shelf costs to do what looks like the same job.

That question has a real answer, and this guide is built to give it to you. But it starts with an honest structural problem: **nobody in the installed tier publishes a price.** Invisible Fence Brand's own cost page carries no installed figure at all — each professional tier ends in a quote request and a disclaimer that services and pricing may vary by location due to regional differences in property types, installation requirements, and local regulations. The only price printed on that page belongs to the company's do-it-yourself tier, a GPS collar from $300, which is precisely not the installed system people are asking about. So there is no manufacturer figure for the installed job to compare against, and any guide that hands you a precise installed price for a branded system is inventing it.

What does exist is a set of published cost estimates from home-services publishers, and a set of published recurring plan prices from the manufacturer itself. Those are what this guide uses, attributed source by source, and read as estimates rather than quotes.

## What the installed tier costs, according to the publishers who report it

**Angi** reports that invisible fence installation cost averages $1,600, with most homeowners paying between $950 and $2,500 for a professionally installed system, and puts underground systems at $2 to $4 per linear foot. It breaks the same figure down by yard: 400 to 800 linear feet at $700 to $1,200, 800 to 1,200 feet at $1,100 to $1,800, 1,200 to 1,800 feet at $1,500 to $2,500, and over 1,800 feet at $2,000 to $3,500. By system type it reports wired at around $1,000, wireless at around $350, and GPS-based at around $2,000.

**HomeGuide** reports installing an invisible dog fence at $800 to $2,800 on average, or $2 to $7 per linear foot, and states that an electric fence for dogs costs $1,600 to $2,000 per acre — noting an acre needs 850 to 900 linear feet of boundary wire for a rectangular yard.

**HomeAdvisor** reports electric fence installation at $1,337 on average, with a low end around $425 and a high end around $2,042, and puts underground invisible fence work at $2 to $6 per linear foot or $1,000 to $5,000 per acre.

**Bob Vila** gives the widest band, reporting invisible fence cost at $200 to $2,500 depending on the size and number of pets, at $2.40 to $3.90 per linear foot — and adds the thing most cost tables leave out, that homeowners also need to factor in the cost of training the dog to use the fence.

Three caveats belong with those numbers rather than in a footnote. First, **Angi and HomeAdvisor are properties of the same company** and should not be counted as two independent confirmations of each other. Second, these publishers **personalize their headline figures by metro**, so the range you see on their sites may differ from the range quoted here. Third, and most important: **these ranges describe electronic pet fencing generally, not the branded dealer-installed tier specifically.** No publisher we could verify breaks out branded dealer pricing at all.

## The recurring cost the sticker price never shows

This is the part of the installed tier that is genuinely published, and it is the most useful number in this guide. Invisible Fence Brand's battery and service plans page lists a **Complete Care Plan at $399.00** (battery plan plus service plan), a **Power Cap battery plan at $99.00**, and an **Annual Service Plan at $329.00** — the last described as saving approximately $185 on a typical service call.

Read that against the DIY column. The Complete Care Plan costs $399.00 a year. The PetSafe Guardian at the top of this guide costs $399.99 once, and PetSafe states it carries no subscription or fee at all because it has no tracking service to bill for. That is not a small difference of degree. Over five years it is the difference between one payment of $399.99 and roughly $2,000 in plan renewals on top of an installed cost of $950 to $2,500.

Angi's own ongoing-cost figures fill in the DIY side of the same ledger honestly: replacement batteries at $10 to $60 per year, GPS subscriptions at $5 to $15 per month where a system requires one, wire-break repairs at $100 to $300, transmitter issues at $75 to $200, and collar replacement at $50 to $150. HomeGuide reports technician fees to test and flag a perimeter at $150 to $250. Owning the system yourself means owning those repairs.

## So what does the professional premium actually buy?

Not a boundary that holds better. The containment technology in a dealer-installed in-ground system is the same continuous-loop buried wire you can buy in a box, and the GPS tier is the same satellite boundary.

What it buys is stated plainly by the company selling it, and taking it seriously is the fair thing to do. Invisible Fence Brand lists **three Perfect Start Plus training sessions led by certified trainers**, a **one-year Complete Care Plan** of free service calls for wire breaks, boundary flagging and battery replacements, a **limited lifetime warranty** on its premium in-ground tier, and indoor Shields avoidance. It draws the DIY contrast itself, writing that unlike other DIY dog fences and underground fences, its professional, animal-behaviorist-backed pet training helps a pet feel confident in the yard, meaning fewer break-outs and less stress.

That claim about fewer break-outs is the company's own, not an independent finding, and it should be read as marketing. But the mechanism behind it is not silly. Training is the part of this category that DIY buyers most often skip, and it is the part that decides whether the system works.

There is one more claim on the same pages worth flagging rather than repeating: the company describes itself as the number-one dog fence recommended by veterinarians, and publishes no survey, sample or source for that anywhere. Given the veterinary-behavior positions quoted in the next section, treat it as an advertising claim until the data behind it is published.

## The finding that outranks the price question

Before the money matters, this does. **Starinsky, Lord and Herron's 2017 study in the Journal of the American Veterinary Medical Association** surveyed 974 owners of 1,053 dogs and reported that dogs confined by an electronic fence were more likely to have escaped — 66 of 150, or **44.0%** — than dogs confined by a see-through fence (153 of 658, **23.3%**), a privacy fence (38 of 163, **23.3%**), or a tether (22 of 82, **26.8%**).

Roughly twice as many dogs get through an electronic boundary as get through a fence they can see. That is the single most important number on this page, it applies equally to the $309.71 kit and the $2,500 installation, and no dealer's brochure contains it.

The same study also found that **containment method was not significantly associated with whether dogs had ever bitten** a person or another dog — a result worth stating clearly so this research is not misused. It is evidence about escapes, not about aggression.

On the welfare side, the guidance is consistent and it is not favourable to this category. **AVSAB's 2021 Humane Dog Training Position Statement** states that the application of aversive methods — which by definition rely on application of force, pain, or emotional or physical discomfort — should not be used in canine training or for the treatment of behavioral disorders, and that there is no evidence that aversive training is necessary for dog training or behavior modification. Its FAQ names electronic shock collars explicitly among tools an appropriate trainer should avoid, and it documents the fallout as increased anxiety and fear-related aggression, avoidance, and learned helplessness. AVSAB's board statement on the same page goes further, stating that AVSAB does not support the use of aversives in any context. The **AAHA's Canine and Feline Behavior Management Guidelines** list electronic shock collars among aversive techniques and state that none of those tools and methods should be used to either teach or alter behavior.

Every product in this guide, at every price point, uses static correction. We are not going to sell past that. If it is decisive for you, a physical fence is the answer, and this page has done its job by telling you so.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How much does an Invisible Fence Brand system cost?**
A: The company does not publish an installed price. Its cost page routes you to a local team for a quote on each professional tier and states that services and pricing may vary by location due to regional differences in property types, installation requirements, and local regulations. Cost publishers report the wider professionally installed electronic-fence market at $950 to $2,500 (Angi), $800 to $2,800 (HomeGuide) and around $1,337 on average (HomeAdvisor), but none of them breaks out the branded dealer tier specifically. The company does print one hardware price on that page — its own do-it-yourself GPS collar, listed from $300 — alongside a financing illustration built around a $5,500 example purchase. The first is a different product from an installed system; the second is an illustration rather than a price.

**Q: Is a DIY invisible fence as good as a professionally installed one?**
A: For containment, the technology is the same — the same continuous-loop buried wire, or the same satellite boundary. What differs is training and service. The professional package includes certified-trainer sessions and a service contract for wire breaks; a DIY kit includes neither, and you run the multi-week training window yourself. If you will genuinely do that training, the containment outcome should be comparable. If you will not, the professional route is buying you the thing you would otherwise skip.

**Q: What does an invisible fence cost per linear foot?**
A: The publishers disagree, so here is the spread rather than a single figure. Angi reports $2 to $4 per linear foot for underground systems and puts trenching and wire installation labor in the same $2 to $4 band, with hourly labor at $50 to $100. HomeAdvisor reports $2 to $6 per linear foot. HomeGuide reports $2 to $7. Bob Vila reports $2.40 to $3.90. Note that Angi and HomeAdvisor belong to the same company, so the genuinely independent estimates here are fewer than the number of sources suggests.

**Q: Are there ongoing costs after the fence is installed?**
A: Yes, on both routes. Invisible Fence Brand publishes a Complete Care Plan at $399.00 a year, an Annual Service Plan at $329.00, and a Power Cap battery plan at $99.00 — and states the service plan saves approximately $185 on a typical service call, which tells you roughly what an out-of-plan visit costs. On the DIY side, Angi reports replacement batteries at $10 to $60 a year, GPS subscriptions at $5 to $15 a month where required, and wire-break repairs at $100 to $300. The one structure with no recurring cost at all is a no-subscription system you maintain yourself, which is why the PetSafe Guardian is the top pick here.

**Q: Do invisible fences actually keep dogs in?**
A: Less reliably than a physical fence, according to the best non-vendor data available. The 2017 JAVMA study by Starinsky, Lord and Herron reported that 44.0% of dogs contained by an electronic fence had escaped, against 23.3% for a see-through fence and 23.3% for a privacy fence. HomeGuide states the caution on its own cost page, noting that electric fences may not effectively contain dogs and can cause behavioral issues, and advising readers to consult a vet and check local ordinances, since some local laws prohibit them.

**Q: Can I install a buried-wire fence myself?**
A: Yes — it is the labor, not the skill, that stops most people. The SportDOG kit in this guide ships 1,000 feet of wire, two collars and 100 flags, and covers 1 1/3 acres out of the box. Burying that much wire around a real property line is a weekend of trenching. At the $2 to $4 per linear foot Angi reports for trenching and wire installation, that same 1,000 feet would represent roughly $2,000 to $4,000 of contracted labor. The trade you are making is a weekend against that number, plus owning any future wire-break repair yourself.

**Q: Which is cheaper over five years, professional or DIY?**
A: DIY, in almost every scenario we can construct from published figures — but the gap depends entirely on subscriptions. A no-fee system like the PetSafe Guardian is $399.99 and then nothing. A professionally installed system at Angi's reported $950 to $2,500, plus five renewals of a $399.00 Complete Care Plan, lands in the $2,900 to $4,500 region. A DIY system with a required subscription sits in between, and the gap narrows fast with more than one dog. Our [GPS Dog Fence Total Cost of Ownership](/guides/best-gps-dog-containment-total-cost-comparison-2026) guide runs those subscription totals properly from each brand's published plan rates.

**Q: Does an invisible fence work for a dog that already escapes?**
A: This is the case where we would tell you not to buy one. The JAVMA escape figures describe dogs in general; a dog with a history of running through boundaries is self-selected into the harder half of that distribution. An electronic boundary is a deterrent, and a dog willing to take a correction to chase something will take it. For that dog, layer the containment — our [How to Create a Safe Dog Backyard](/guides/how-to-create-a-safe-dog-backyard-containment-2026) guide covers pairing a boundary with a physical kennel, a dig barrier and a tracker — or use a physical fence.
