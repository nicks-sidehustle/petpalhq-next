A leash is the piece of gear a dog uses on every walk, and the right one depends on the job. This guide picks seven leashes by use: an everyday leash, a reflective rope leash for low light, two hands-free leashes for running and hiking, a short traffic lead for close control, a long line for recall practice, and a classic six-foot rope leash. Retractable leashes are left out, for reasons the AKC explains below.

Start with the baseline. The AKC's advice on retractables says: "Until your dog understands how to walk politely using a standard six-foot leash, hold off on the retractable version." Every pick here is a fixed-length leash, and two of them are hands-free versions of one.

## Pick the Leash for the Job

Mendota Pet's leash-choosing guide sorts leashes by length, and the picks here follow the same logic. A traffic lead keeps a dog close in a city, at crowded events or around cars and doors. A six-foot lead is Mendota's everyday length. Leads of 8 ft to 15 ft give room on hikes and in the field and can be draped around the waist. Longer lines are for field training and teaching a dog to come.

Hands-free leashes are a separate choice. The Roamer and the Flagline both buckle around the waist, and both suit a dog that already moves steadily beside you. A study of US emergency-department data from 2001 to 2018, published in the American Journal of Emergency Medicine, found: "The most common type of dog leash-related injuries resulted from a pull followed by a trip/tangle." That study covers all leash types, not one design, and it also found that "The number of dog leash-related injuries increased during the time period." Keep a waist-worn leash for a dog that does not lunge.

The clip matters as much as the length. Three picks here have a clip the maker or listing calls locking: the Knot-a-Leash, the Front Range Leash and the Flagline. The others use a snap or a swiveling clip with no lock described.

## Walking After Dark

Zoetis Petcare, a commercial pet-health publisher, says: "Dogs should have a reflective leash and collar." It cites NHTSA: "According to NHTSA, three out of every four pedestrian fatalities occur at night, and visibility plays a critical role in these incidents." The Knot-a-Leash is the only pick here described as reflective. The others are not, so for night walks pair them with reflective gear on the dog and on you.

## Retractable Leashes: What the AKC Says

The AKC does not ban retractable leashes; it explains when and how to use them safely. Its cautions are specific. On length: "Retractable leashes can generally be anywhere between 10 and 30 feet, but 16 feet is the most common length." On injury: "When your dog hits the end of the leash, your dog can suffer injuries like a damaged trachea or spine." On training: "Unfortunately, a retractable leash rewards pulling." On visibility: "Thin cords are hard to see, particularly at night." If you do buy one, the AKC's advice is: "Look for a tape leash rather than a cord, preferably one that’s a bright color or has reflective markings." Crowds are on its list of times to use a standard leash instead.

Some trainers go further. The trainer who wrote Whole Dog Journal's long-line article says: "I don’t use retractables for many reasons, including the fact that it teaches a dog that constant leash pressure is normal." For room to roam with a fixed length, a long line like the Mendota Long Snap does the job a retractable is often bought for.

## Long Lines and Recall

Whole Dog Journal's trainer calls the long line "an incredible tool for teaching a terrific recall", and starts its recall exercise with a "20-footer, great treats, big park, and a sniffari." The Mendota Long Snap is 15 ft, shorter than that example line, so it gives a little less room. Use any long line in open space, away from traffic, and keep it off sidewalks.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What length of dog leash is best for everyday walks?**
A: The AKC uses a "standard six-foot leash" as its benchmark, and Mendota Pet calls the 6 ft lead ideal for everyday walking. The Mendota Snap Leash on this page is 6 ft, and the Ruffwear Front Range Leash is 5 ft.

**Q: Are retractable leashes safe?**
A: The AKC explains how to use them safely rather than ruling them out, but it lists real risks: a dog that hits the end of the leash can suffer injuries like a damaged trachea or spine, a retractable rewards pulling, and thin cords are hard to see at night. It says to hold off on one until your dog walks politely on a standard six-foot leash. One trainer writing for Whole Dog Journal does not use retractables at all.

**Q: Is a hands-free leash a good idea?**
A: For a dog that already walks or runs steadily beside you, it can be. Ruffwear designs the Roamer and Flagline to be worn around the waist. Mendota Pet's leash-choosing guide cautions that a dog prone to lunging or weaving around your legs could trip you, so hold the leash in your hand until your dog is steady.

**Q: Which leash is best for walking at night?**
A: A reflective one. Zoetis Petcare says dogs should have a reflective leash and collar, and the Ruffwear Knot-a-Leash is the only pick here described as reflective.

**Q: What is a traffic lead for?**
A: Keeping a dog right at your side. Mendota's Traffic Leash is a short 1.5 foot handled lead, and Mendota names city walks, crowded events, training and dogs that bolt from cars or doors as its uses. The Ruffwear Front Range Leash and Roamer build a traffic handle into a full-length leash instead.
