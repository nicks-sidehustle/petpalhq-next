whenNotToBuy: |
  Skip containment entirely if your yard is under three-quarters of an acre. PetSafe publishes that floor for its no-subscription Guardian, and it reflects the precision limit of satellite positioning rather than a product tier. On a small lot the boundary drifts into space the dog is supposed to be allowed in, which is worse than no fence at all.

  Skip containment if the escape does not happen in the yard. A dog that bolts through the front door, slips a lead on a walk, or goes over a gate that was left open is not a containment problem, and a collar that enforces a back-garden boundary will not touch any of those. That is the tracker case, and it is the more common one.

  Skip a static-correction system if your dog has a noise phobia or high prey drive as its primary escape trigger. VCA Animal Hospitals describes the failure mode directly: a dog that sees something it really wants to chase, or is frightened by thunder or something inside the yard, may breach the fence and ignore the stimulus. What you are left with is a dog outside the boundary and no map.

  Skip subscription-dependent containment in a multi-dog household unless you have priced it per collar. Halo's shop page states that a plan is required for each collar, so two dogs is two permanent monthly charges rather than one shared one.

  Skip both categories if what you actually need is a physical fence. Neither product type stops anything entering the yard — VCA is explicit that invisible fences do not prevent hazards from entering, and that stray dogs and wild animals can still get in and interact aggressively with the resident dog.

Two products in this category are sold with almost the same words and solve opposite halves of the same problem. A GPS fence tries to stop the dog leaving. A GPS tracker tells you where the dog went. Tractive even calls its geofencing feature a Virtual Fence, and its own page describes the mechanism as an instant alert to your phone — there is no correction and no containment anywhere in it.

So the first decision is not which brand. It is which half of the problem you are buying.

## The Four Questions That Decide It

Answer these in order. In most households the answer falls out before question four.

1. **Where does the escape actually happen?** In the yard, or through the front door, over a gate, or off a lead on a walk? Only the first is a containment problem. The rest are recovery problems, and a back-garden boundary does nothing about them.
2. **How big is the property?** PetSafe publishes its no-subscription Guardian for properties of three-quarters of an acre or larger. That floor is a physical limit on satellite precision, not a product tier — below it, a virtual boundary drifts into space the dog is meant to be allowed in.
3. **How big is the dog?** The Guardian is designed for dogs over 25 pounds with 13-to-28-inch necks. SpotOn publishes a floor of 15 pounds with a 10-to-26-inch neck. Halo publishes over 10 pounds with an 8-to-30-inch neck. Below roughly 10 pounds this category has no answer.
4. **Will you accept a recurring bill on the safety function?** This is the fork inside containment, and it is worth more than any spec on the box.

## When the Answer Is a Fence

If the yard is the failure point, the property clears three-quarters of an acre, and the dog clears the weight floor, containment is the right purchase — and the choice inside it is almost entirely commercial.

- **SpotOn Nova, $999.00.** The fence works with no plan. SpotOn's subscription page says that without a plan you can still create GPS fences and train your dog to stay in; what a plan adds is location and tracking. Its product page adds that standard features including unlimited fences require neither cellular service nor Wi-Fi.
- **Halo Collar 5, $524.00.** The fence is part of the subscription. Halo's home page states a Pack Membership Plan is required to activate and maintain GPS services and to create, edit, and use wireless dog fences. Published tiers start at $9.99 a month, and a plan is required for each collar.
- **PetSafe Guardian, $399.99.** "No subscription fees or hidden costs – ever," in PetSafe's own words, with no tracking of any kind in exchange.

The mistake to avoid here is buying on sticker price. A $524.00 collar with a permanent per-collar subscription and a $999.00 collar with none are not the same purchase separated by $475.

## When the Answer Is a Tracker

If the yard already holds, the fence is an expensive answer to a question you are not asking. Recovery is what is missing, and it costs a fraction as much.

A tracker's specific strength is that it is not tied to one property. It works on a walk, at a relative's house, at a campsite, and in the car — every place a containment collar drawn around your own garden is irrelevant. Its specific weakness is that it prevents nothing at all, and it depends on mobile coverage wherever the dog actually went.

Tractive publishes its plans openly: Basic at $9 a month on a one-year term or $6 on a two-year, Premium at $10, $7, and $5 a month on one-, two-, and five-year terms. Fi bundles twelve months with the Series 3+ collar and publishes a $19 monthly rate in its own site title — but no discounted term structure anywhere we could load on August 21, 2026, so its five-year figure is 48 months at full price. Fi has also changed address: tryfi.com now returns an HTTP 301 to fitracking.com, which is where that rate is published.

## When Buying Both Is the Right Answer

For a serious repeat escape artist, the honest recommendation is one of each, and it is cheaper than it looks.

A PetSafe Guardian and a Tractive together are $478.99 on day one — less than a Halo collar plus its first year of entry membership. You get containment that never lapses and recovery that works anywhere, in exchange for two collars to fit and two batteries to charge.

It becomes overkill when the yard is already physically fenced. At that point the containment half is redundant and the money belongs entirely in the tracker.

## What Neither One Fixes

Both categories share a limitation that no upgrade within either closes. VCA Animal Hospitals states it plainly: invisible fences protect dogs by preventing them from leaving the yard, but they do not prevent hazards from entering it, and stray dogs and wild animals can still access the yard and interact aggressively with the resident dog.

Nor is a virtual boundary absolute for the dog it addresses. VCA describes the failure directly — a dog that sees something it really wants to chase, or is frightened by thunder or something inside the yard, may breach the fence and ignore the stimulus.

And there is a welfare position on the correction itself that belongs in the decision rather than after it. The AAHA's 2015 Canine and Feline Behavior Management Guidelines list electronic shock collars among aversive techniques and state that none of those tools and methods should be used to teach or alter behavior. AVSAB's 2021 position statement recommends reward-based training methods only and says trainers should avoid tools that involve pain, naming electronic shock collars. The RSPCA notes the electric shock can cause physical problems and behaviour issues linked with pain, fear and stress. None of those statements addresses GPS containment specifically, and the tool class is the same one. A tracker involves no stimulus at all.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is a GPS tracker with a virtual fence the same as a GPS fence?**

A: No, and this is the single most expensive confusion in the category. Tractive's Virtual Fences page describes the mechanism as an instant alert to your phone when the pet crosses a safe zone boundary. There is no correction, no stimulus, and no containment. A GPS fence collar attempts to hold the boundary; a tracker's virtual fence tells you the boundary was crossed.

**Q: Which is cheaper over five years, a fence or a tracker?**

A: On published rates, the tracker — a Tractive at $79.00 with a five-year Premium plan totals $379.00, while the cheapest containment option, the PetSafe Guardian, is $399.99. The gap widens sharply against subscription-dependent containment: a Halo Collar 5 on the entry Bronze tier is $1,198.40 over the same period at its $599.00 list price, built from Halo's own published $9.99 monthly rate — $1,123.40 if the current $524.00 promotional hardware price holds.

**Q: My yard is a quarter of an acre. Can I use a GPS fence?**

A: Not reliably. PetSafe publishes its no-subscription Guardian for properties of three-quarters of an acre or larger, and that floor reflects how precisely satellite positioning can hold a line. On a quarter-acre lot the boundary can drift into areas the dog is supposed to be allowed in, which means corrections in places the dog has done nothing wrong. Physical fencing plus a tracker is the better answer at that size.

**Q: Do I need a subscription for a GPS dog fence?**

A: It depends entirely on the brand, which is why this is the decision rather than a footnote. SpotOn says its fences work without a plan and sells tracking as the add-on. PetSafe's Guardian page says there are no subscription fees or hidden costs, ever. Halo's home page says a Pack Membership Plan is required to create, edit, and use wireless dog fences, and its shop page says a plan is required for each collar.

**Q: What if my dog escapes from the front door rather than the yard?**

A: Then containment is the wrong purchase and a tracker is the right one. A GPS fence enforces a boundary you draw around a property; it has no role in a dog that bolts past you at the door, slips a lead, or goes through a gate a delivery driver left open. Buy the recovery layer and put the difference toward a door routine.

**Q: Can I use a GPS fence for a small dog?**

A: Only above the published floors, and they are higher than most people expect. The PetSafe Guardian is designed for dogs over 25 pounds; SpotOn publishes a 15-pound floor with a 10-to-26-inch neck; Halo publishes over 10 pounds with an 8-to-30-inch neck. Below roughly 10 pounds this category has no product that should be on the dog, and a tracker on a properly fitted harness is the sensible alternative.
