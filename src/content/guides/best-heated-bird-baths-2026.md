Birds still need water when the yard freezes. The National Audubon Society lists three ways to keep it open: "Keep the water thawed by adding warm or hot water, making a hole in the ice, or using a simple birdbath heater." Project FeederWatch, run by the Cornell Lab of Ornithology, puts it simply: "In freezing climates, a birdbath heater will keep ice from forming." This guide covers wild backyard birds, not caged pets, and the six picks are the two kinds of heater Audubon describes: drop-in de-icers for a bath you already own, and plastic baths with the heater built in.

## Heated Bath or Drop-In De-Icer

Audubon gives the two routes in one line: "Instead, purchase a plastic birdbath with a built-in heater, or convert a summer birdbath by adding a heater." A de-icer is the smaller change if your bath is plastic or concrete. The API Bird Bath De-Icer, the API BDT250 and the K&H Super Ice Eliminator all drop into an existing bath. Miller says the API de-icer suits pottery, plastic and concrete baths of 15 gallons or less, Miller says the BDT250 is safe in plastic baths, and K&H says its Ice Eliminators won't melt plastic baths.

A heated bath makes sense if your summer bath is ceramic or you are starting from scratch. The K&H Thermo-Birdbath sits on the ground, the API 600 mounts to a deck rail, pedestal or pole, and the API bath with stand comes with its own 30-inch stand. For a summer bath with moving water, see the [solar bird bath fountain guide](/guides/best-bird-baths-solar-fountains-2026).

## Winter Water Safety

A heater in water is an electrical appliance outdoors, and the safety rules come first. The National Wildlife Federation says: "If using a heater, add a ground-fault interrupted circuit (available at hardware stores) to prevent electric shorts." Miller's own instructions for its item 650 heated bath say the same: "Use a Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter (GFCI) for safety." The NWF adds: "Check that cords and outlets are sheltered from snow or ice buildup." Most picks here have short cords, from 15 to 18 inches, so plan the bath's spot around a protected outlet.

Never use chemicals to stop the freeze. Project FeederWatch says "Never add anti-freeze; it is poisonous to all animals, including birds." The NWF warns against glycerin as well: "Avoid adding glycerin to a birdbath as antifreeze; if birds ingest too much, it can dangerously elevate their blood-sugar levels."

Mind the bath material. Audubon says "Avoid ceramic baths; they can crack in cold weather." And mind the water level: K&H says its Ice Eliminator "is designed to be submerged in water at all times."

## Keeping a Heated Bath Clean and Full

Heated water goes fast in winter. Audubon says "Frequent refills are necessary in winter because the water quickly evaporates in dry air." Audubon's Great Lakes office adds: "Freshen the water every other day, if possible." For cleaning, the same page says: "Clean bird baths with a scrub-brush in a solution that is 9 parts water and 1 part vinegar." Unplug a de-icer before you clean around it; K&H recommends unplugging its Ice Eliminator for cleaning.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Do birds really need water in winter?**
A: Yes. Audubon lists a birdbath heater among the ways to keep water thawed, and Project FeederWatch says "In freezing climates, a birdbath heater will keep ice from forming." Audubon's Great Lakes office notes that "Heated birdbaths can also be purchased at stores."

**Q: Should I buy a heated bath or a de-icer?**
A: If you already own a plastic or concrete bath, a de-icer is the simpler change, and Audubon names converting a summer bath with a heater as one of its two routes. If your bath is ceramic, buy a heated bath instead: Audubon says ceramic baths can crack in cold weather.

**Q: Can I add antifreeze or glycerin to keep the water open?**
A: No. Project FeederWatch says "Never add anti-freeze; it is poisonous to all animals, including birds." The National Wildlife Federation warns that glycerin can dangerously raise birds' blood-sugar levels if they drink too much.

**Q: Do I need a GFCI outlet for a heated bird bath?**
A: Yes. The National Wildlife Federation says to add a ground-fault interrupted circuit with any bath heater to prevent electric shorts, and Miller's instructions for its item 650 heated bath say to use a GFCI. Keep cords and outlets sheltered from snow and ice.

**Q: What happens if a de-icer runs dry?**
A: Check each maker's instructions. K&H says its Ice Eliminator is designed to be submerged at all times and to unplug it if the bath will run dry on a regular basis. Audubon warns that winter water evaporates quickly in dry air, so check the level often.

**Q: How often should I clean a heated bird bath?**
A: Audubon's Great Lakes office suggests freshening the water every other day if possible and scrubbing the bath with a solution of 9 parts water to 1 part vinegar. Unplug a de-icer before cleaning around it.
