Litter box furniture solves a human problem: the box is in the living room and nobody wants to look at it. Whether it also works for the cat depends mostly on one number: the interior. The 2014 AAFP/ISFM house-soiling guidelines recommend a rectangular box about 1.5 times the length of the cat from nose to base of tail, and they cite a study in which adult cats preferred a 34-by-15-inch box over a smaller one. An enclosure can only hold a box as big as its inside, so a handsome cabinet with a cramped compartment turns a good box into a small covered one.

That matters because the veterinary sources are cautious about covers. AAFP/ISFM reports that cats are equally divided between large covered boxes and uncovered ones, so an enclosure is not a lost cause, but it warns that small covered boxes may make it hard for larger cats to posture normally. Cornell adds that covers hold odors in from the cat's point of view. VCA flags the exit of a covered pan as an ambush point when other cats share the house, and the ASPCA says cats like multiple escape routes.

So this guide ranks enclosures on what they do, read from their listings: interior size first, then the cat's way in and out, then ventilation, moisture resistance and cleaning access, then how well the piece works as furniture and what it costs. Seven enclosures made the cut, from a two-in-one cat tree to a cabinet big enough for an automatic box.

These picks are editorial synthesis. PetPalHQ does not run a testing lab; every product was chosen by reading manufacturer-supplied Amazon listings against AAFP/ISFM, Cornell, VCA and ASPCA guidance. Prices change, so check the current price on Amazon before you buy.

## What Should You Measure Before Buying an Enclosure?

Three things, in order. First the cat, from the nose to the base of the tail. Multiply by 1.5 and you have the box length AAFP/ISFM recommends. Then the box you plan to use, including the rim. Then the enclosure's interior, not its outside dimensions. The box has to fit inside with room for the cat to step in, turn and dig.

Four picks here list an interior with a longest side of 27 inches or more: the Amunrbrek, the MEEXPAWS bench, the unipaws cabinet, and the Feandrea end table with its divider out. If you still need to choose the box itself, [Best Standard Litter Boxes for Cats](/guides/best-standard-litter-boxes-2026) ranks pans by usable size.

## What Does an Enclosure Cost the Cat?

Mostly air and escape routes. Cornell's point is that a cover improves the room's air and worsens the cat's, and VCA says covered pans may hold in odors that are associated with infrequent cleaning. Ventilation holes help at the margin; the Amunrbrek, the ONBRILL cabinet and the unipaws cabinet all have them on the back. The real fix is a scoop, used often. For accidents that have already soaked in, [our odor remover guide](/guides/best-pet-odor-removers-2026) covers the enzyme cleaners.

The exit is the other cost. The ASPCA notes that cats like to see animals approaching and like multiple escape routes, and a cabinet with one doorway gives them neither. In a single-cat home that is usually fine. In a multi-cat home, read [How to Set Up Litter Boxes for Multiple Cats](/guides/how-to-set-up-litter-boxes-for-multiple-cats-2026) before you hide every box behind a door.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How big should the inside of a litter box enclosure be?**
A: Big enough for a box about 1.5 times your cat's length from nose to tail base, plus room to step in and turn. That is the AAFP/ISFM ratio, so a cat measuring 18 inches needs a box about 27 inches long, and the enclosure's interior has to be longer still. Read the interior figure, not the outside dimensions. For the New Age Pet Litter Loo, whose listing gives the whole piece as 30" × 24" × 28.9", measure the inside on arrival before you commit to a box.

**Q: Will my cat use a litter box inside furniture?**
A: Many will, but it is not guaranteed. AAFP/ISFM reports that cats are equally divided in their preference for large, covered litter boxes versus uncovered boxes, and the word that carries the weight there is large. The same guidelines warn that small covered boxes may make it difficult for larger cats to posture normally. An enclosure with a cramped compartment is the case to avoid, not enclosures in general.

**Q: Do litter box enclosures stop the smell?**
A: They move it. An enclosure keeps odor out of the room, but Cornell says covers hold odors in from the cat's side, and VCA links covered-pan odor to infrequent cleaning. Rear ventilation holes, as on the Amunrbrek, the ONBRILL cabinet and the unipaws cabinet, let some air out, and a moisture-resistant build like the New Age Pet ECOFLEX keeps spills from soaking into the furniture. None of that replaces scooping.

**Q: Is a two-cat enclosure a good idea for a multi-cat home?**
A: Only if the cats already share calmly. AAFP/ISFM warns that in multi-cat households, despot cats may prevent timid cats from accessing or exiting the litter box, and VCA notes a covered pan may let other cats ambush a cat as it exits. Two rooms in one cabinet sit in one spot, so a cat that guards one door can watch both.

**Q: Can I put a self-cleaning litter box inside an enclosure?**
A: Some enclosures are built for it. The Amunrbrek's listing says its 30-inch-tall interior accommodates most self-cleaning and automatic litter boxes, but check your machine's own dimensions against it before ordering. If you are still choosing the machine, [the self-cleaning litter box guide](/guides/best-self-cleaning-litter-boxes-large-multi-cat-2026) covers large and multi-cat models.

**Q: Are enclosures suitable for kittens or senior cats?**
A: Check the opening before anything else. Cornell says kittens and elderly cats need boxes with low sides, and an enclosure adds its own threshold on top of the box's rim. Measure how high the cat's opening sits above the floor when the enclosure arrives, and watch how the cat handles the step.
