## Why Does Box Size Decide Almost Everything?

Because a cat that cannot turn around, dig, and posture comfortably inside a box will eventually find somewhere it can. The AAFP/ISFM guidelines tie box dimensions directly to the cat's body length rather than to a product category, which is the only sensible way to spec a box: a Maine Coon and a six-pound rescue are not shopping in the same aisle even though the store stocks one shelf. Cornell, the ASPCA and the Merck Veterinary Manual all converge on the same practical conclusion in plainer words — larger boxes are better, and most cats prefer them.

What makes this an expensive mistake is that it presents as a behavioral problem. An owner sees puddles on a bath mat and starts buying enzyme cleaner, rehoming furniture, or reading about stress. Meanwhile the cause may be a pan two inches too short for the cat to stand in without her tail touching the wall. The [multi-cat setup guide](/guides/how-to-set-up-litter-boxes-for-multiple-cats-2026) covers the logistics side of this, but the sizing fix is usually simpler and cheaper than anything else you will try.

Depth matters too, and here more is not better. AAFP/ISFM specifies at least 1.25 inches of litter, and the ASPCA and Cornell both land on 1 to 2 inches as the working range, with the ASPCA explicitly warning against the three-to-four-inch drifts some owners maintain out of good intentions.

## Open, Hooded, Top-Entry or Sifting: Which Type Should You Buy?

Open, unless you have a specific reason not to be. The ASPCA's guidance is unusually direct on this point — remove covers and liners from all litter boxes, because most cats dislike both. AAFP/ISFM is gentler, reporting that cats in research were roughly evenly split between covered and open boxes, but the guideline still recommends open boxes wherever possible, and names its two reasons: to facilitate owner monitoring and frequent scooping. It adds that an open box avoids trapping odors inside, and scopes its posture warning specifically to small covered boxes, which it says may make it difficult for larger cats to posture normally.

Hoods and domes solve a household problem rather than a cat problem. They contain scatter, hide the contents from guests, and take a charcoal filter. Cornell's framing is the one to keep in mind — covers hold odors in, which reads as a benefit from the sofa and a penalty from inside the box. VCA adds a second objection specific to multi-cat homes: an enclosed box has only one exit, which lets one cat trap another inside. Whether a cat then abandons the box is our inference; the single-exit risk is the part VCA states.

Top-entry designs are the most aggressive tracking control available without a motor, and they carry the steepest access cost. A cat has to jump up, drop in, and climb out. That is fine for a healthy three-year-old and disqualifying for a stiff thirteen-year-old. Sifting boxes are a different axis entirely — they change the owner's workflow rather than the cat's experience, and they only work with clumping litter.

## What Changes When a Cat Gets Old or Arthritic?

The rim becomes the whole product. Merck states that older cats with arthritis might need litter boxes with lower sides so that they can enter and exit easily, and VCA says a larger box with lower sides might be necessary for a cat with arthritis, declining sensory function or cognitive decline. Cornell states the general version: elderly cats and cats with mobility problems need boxes with low sides. All three put that advice inside guidance about cats who stop using the box, so treat it as a mechanical cause to rule out rather than as senior pampering — and it is fixable for about fifty dollars.

Watch for the pattern rather than waiting for certainty. A cat who has used the same box for a decade, who now urinates beside it rather than in it, who hesitates at the rim, or who has stopped jumping onto the sofa she used to own, is telling you something mechanical. A low-entry box placed on the same floor the cat already spends time on removes both the climb and the stairs in one move.

Do the medical work in parallel, though. Arthritis and lower urinary tract disease look identical from the hallway carpet, and every source in this guide treats sudden house soiling as a veterinary workup before it is a shopping decision.

## How Many Boxes Does the House Actually Need?

One more than you have cats, in at least two separate locations. That rule is stated independently by AAFP/ISFM, Cornell, the ASPCA, Merck and VCA, which is about as close to settled as household cat advice gets. The ASPCA gives the worked example: three cats means a minimum of four boxes. VCA adds the clause that matters in a house with stairs — spreading boxes across locations protects a cat who cannot safely cross another cat's territory to reach the only option.

Placement is the quieter half of the equation. The Ohio State Indoor Pet Initiative warns against siting boxes near noisy appliances or air ducts, and against spots where a cat can be cornered with no line of retreat. A box beside a furnace that kicks on mid-visit teaches a cat exactly one lesson, and the guides on [multi-cat box logistics](/guides/how-to-set-up-litter-boxes-for-multiple-cats-2026) go further into the mapping.

Cleaning cadence is the last non-negotiable. Every main source here specifies a daily scoop; AAFP/ISFM puts a full wash at every one to four weeks, and Merck says at least weekly. Treat weekly as the default and note how much of this guide's scoring is really about whether a design makes that routine easy enough to survive a busy month.

## When Should You Stop Buying Manual Boxes?

When the daily scoop is the constraint rather than the cat. Manual boxes scale by multiplication — more cats means more pans, more floor space and more minutes each morning — and there is a household size, travel schedule or physical limitation past which that arithmetic simply stops working. ## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What size litter box does my cat actually need?**
A: Measure your cat from the nose to the base of the tail and multiply by 1.5. The AAFP/ISFM house-soiling guidelines give that ratio directly, and they cite research in which cats preferred a 34-by-15-inch box over a smaller one. An eighteen-inch cat therefore wants about twenty-seven inches of interior length, which rules out many boxes marketed as large. Measure the interior floor, not the outer shell — the difference between those two numbers is often four or five inches, and it all comes out of the cat's turning room.

**Q: Are covered litter boxes actually bad for cats?**
A: Bad is too strong, but the guidance leans clearly toward open pans. The ASPCA advises removing covers and liners from all boxes, noting that most cats dislike both. AAFP/ISFM found cats roughly evenly split on the question, yet still recommends open boxes wherever possible, for two stated reasons: to facilitate owner monitoring and frequent scooping. It also says an open box avoids trapping odors inside, and aims its posture warning at small covered boxes specifically. Cornell makes the same odor point from the cat's side of the lid. VCA adds that an enclosed box has only one exit, which is a real risk in multi-cat homes where one cat guards doorways.

**Q: How many litter boxes should a multi-cat home have?**
A: One per cat plus one more, spread across at least two separate locations. AAFP/ISFM, Cornell, the ASPCA, Merck and VCA all state this independently, which makes it about as reliable as household cat guidance gets. The ASPCA's worked example is three cats and four boxes. The two-location clause matters as much as the count, because a single bank of boxes in one room can still be controlled by one assertive cat, leaving the others with nowhere safe to go.

**Q: My senior cat started going beside the box. Is it the box?**
A: It might be, and it might be a urinary problem, so book the vet visit first. Once medical causes are ruled out, rim height is a mechanical suspect worth checking. Merck says older cats with arthritis might need litter boxes with lower sides so that they can enter and exit easily, VCA says a larger box with lower sides might be necessary for a cat with arthritis, and Cornell states that elderly cats and cats with mobility problems need boxes with low sides. Watch for hesitation at the rim, missed jumps onto furniture, and urination immediately beside the box rather than across the room — that pattern points at access rather than preference.

**Q: How deep should the litter be, and how often should I clean it?**
A: Shallower than most owners assume, and more often than most owners manage. AAFP/ISFM specifies at least 1.25 inches of litter, while the ASPCA and Cornell both land on a 1-to-2-inch working range, with the ASPCA warning specifically against three or four inches. Scoop daily — every source in this guide agrees on that one. Wash the box fully every one to four weeks according to AAFP/ISFM, or at least weekly per Merck, and treat weekly as the safe default.

**Q: Is a sifting litter box worth it compared to scooping?**
A: It depends on which part of the chore you dislike. Sifting swaps clump-hunting with a scoop for lifting a perforated pan and shaking the clean litter through, which some owners find genuinely faster. The trade-offs are real: sifting systems work only with clumping litters, so pellets and paper crumble are out, and lifting a loaded pan is more physical than scooping. If your reason for wanting less scooping is wrist or shoulder pain, sifting may be the wrong direction entirely.

**Q: Should I just buy an automatic litter box instead?**
A: Only if the daily scoop is the real constraint. A correctly sized manual box, scooped daily, with an inch or two of unscented clumping litter in a quiet spot, meets the full veterinary brief for about thirty dollars. The case for automation is narrower and specific: constant travel, a mobility limitation that makes daily scooping unreliable, a serious odor sensitivity, or a cat count high enough that manual scaling has stopped being practical. Buy the machine for one of those reasons, not because the manual box feels unsophisticated.
