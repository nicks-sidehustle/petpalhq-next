A cat who stops jumping onto the bed is usually not slowing down. She is usually in pain, and she is hiding it, because that is what cats evolved to do. The Cornell Feline Health Center puts a number on how common this is: X-rays revealed that 90 percent of cats over the age of 12 showed evidence of degenerative joint disease. The Merck Veterinary Manual gives a similar range, stating that approximately 60% to 90% of older cats have osteoarthritis. VCA Animal Hospitals reports that about 90% of cats over 10 years old experience osteoarthritis in at least one joint. These are three different studies with three different age cutoffs, and they converge on the same uncomfortable conclusion.

The masking is the part owners miss. Cornell explains the behaviour as a deeply ingrained feline instinct for self-protection — a cat may want to conceal its disability for fear of alerting a potential predator to its vulnerability. Merck says the same thing more bluntly: the condition is common in cats but it may not be noticed because cats often hide signs of pain. So the signal is rarely a limp. It is a cat who used to sleep on the bed and now sleeps on the chair, and a household that reads the change as personality rather than arthritis.

That is why this category exists, and VCA recommends it explicitly. Among the home changes it lists for an arthritic cat is a plain instruction: provide a ramp or stool/step for getting onto higher surfaces. This page is the ranked version of that sentence — seven ramps and step sets scored on the six things that decide whether a cat will actually use one.

These picks are editorial synthesis. PetPalHQ does not run a feline mobility testing lab, and nothing here has been tested with a cat on it. ## Why Has Your Cat Stopped Jumping?

Because jumping got expensive, and cats are relentless economists about their own bodies. A healthy cat clears five or six times her own height without appearing to think about it. An arthritic cat still can, once, painfully — so she budgets. The bed becomes a place she visits less often, then a place she visits when someone lifts her, then a place she has given up. Nothing about that sequence looks like an injury from the outside, which is precisely why it gets misread.

VCA's list of signs is the most useful diagnostic checklist an owner can carry, because it describes behaviour rather than pathology. Reluctance to jump up or down, onto or off furniture, sits at the top of it. Hiding more than usual is on it. So is poor coat condition, because self-grooming becomes difficult when twisting hurts. Cornell adds a detail that catches a lot of households by surprise: an affected cat may seem lethargic and may increasingly seek out comforting places to nap, in a warm corner of your home or in a spot of sunshine near a window. If your cat has relocated her entire day to the floor, the floor is not a preference. It is an accommodation she made without telling you.

The scale is worth sitting with. Cornell's second figure is the one that should change how younger households think: another study suggested that 20 percent of all cats one year of age and older are already arthritic to some extent. One cat in five, from the age of one. This is not a condition that arrives at fourteen with a bang; it accumulates quietly for years before the first missed jump.

## Ramp or Steps: Which Does an Arthritic Cat Actually Need?

A ramp asks a cat to walk. Steps ask a cat to jump, repeatedly, in smaller increments. That is the whole distinction, and it explains why both ramps on this page outrank all five stepped products despite costing more and taking up more floor. If the underlying problem is that a single lift has become painful, replacing one large lift with three small ones helps less than removing the lift entirely.

Steps still win on practical grounds often enough to matter. A 62-inch or 70-inch ramp needs a run of clear floor that many bedrooms simply do not have, especially against the side of a bed. Steps occupy a footprint closer to a nightstand. They are also easier to introduce, because a cat can take one step and stop, which is a much smaller commitment than committing to a slope. For a cat in early-stage joint disease who is hesitating rather than failing, a low-rise step set is often the more realistic purchase.

The compromise most households end up making is a ramp at the destination the cat cares about most and steps elsewhere. In practice that means the bed, because that is where the cat wants to be at night and where the height differential is largest. Whichever you choose, the specification that decides everything is the one three of these seven listings never publish: how much vertical the cat has to produce in one movement.

## What Incline Is Safe? Nobody Publishes a Number

Here is the finding that should change how you read every other article on this subject: no veterinary authority we could verify publishes a safe incline gradient or a maximum step rise for cats. We checked Cornell, Merck and VCA directly. VCA recommends a ramp or a step by name and gives no angle and no height. Merck is silent on the question entirely. Cornell covers signs and treatment without touching product geometry.

This matters because the retail internet is full of confident numbers. You will find pages specifying a maximum incline in degrees, or an ideal rise in inches, presented with the tone of established clinical guidance. None of the three veterinary sources behind this guide supports any of them, and we are not going to repeat a figure we cannot attribute. If you see a specific angle quoted somewhere, ask which veterinary body published it, and notice how rarely that question has an answer.

What can be said without a source behind it is modest and obvious: gentler is better, particularly for a cat whose pain is already advanced, and a rise you can see your cat clear comfortably today is more trustworthy than any number. That is why the score below weights published rise height above everything else. Not because a threshold exists, but because a manufacturer who publishes the rise lets you make the judgement yourself, and a manufacturer who does not has handed you a guess.

## How Do You Get a Cat to Use a Ramp?

Slowly, and by not making it a project. Cats are suspicious of new furniture, and a cat in pain is more suspicious than most because unfamiliar surfaces carry risk. The failure mode is an owner who installs a ramp, watches the cat ignore it for two days, and concludes the cat does not need one — when what actually happened is that nothing invited her onto it.

Put it in place before you need it, if you can. The most reliable installations are the ones that went in while the cat could still jump, so the ramp became ordinary furniture rather than an intervention arriving alongside a decline. Feed treats at the bottom of it, then partway up, then at the top, over a week rather than an afternoon. Let the cat approach it with all four feet on the floor, which is how she assesses whether a surface will hold. And if she tries it once, slips, and never returns, that is usually a traction problem rather than a refusal — which is why surface traction is the second-heaviest factor in the score below.

Height matters to cats in a way that is easy to underestimate, and this is the site's own editorial read rather than a sourced claim: the elevated spots a cat chooses are not arbitrary, and losing access to them is a bigger event than losing access to a piece of furniture. When a cat gives up the windowsill, she gives up the view, the sunlight and the vantage point in one move. Restoring that access is worth more patience than most owners give it.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How do I know my cat has arthritis and is not just getting old?**
A: You mostly do not, from the hallway, which is the problem. Cornell reports that 90 percent of cats over the age of 12 show evidence of degenerative joint disease on X-ray, and Merck estimates that approximately 60 to 90 percent of older cats have osteoarthritis, so the base rate is high enough that suspicion is reasonable in any older cat. The behavioural signs VCA lists are reluctance to jump onto or off furniture, hiding more than usual, and poor coat condition because self-grooming becomes difficult. None of that is a diagnosis. A vet with a set of X-rays gives you one, and that visit should come before the shopping.

**Q: What incline is safe for a cat ramp?**
A: No veterinary authority we could verify publishes one. Cornell, Merck and VCA were all checked directly for this guide. VCA recommends providing a ramp or stool or step for getting onto higher surfaces and gives no angle whatsoever; Merck does not address product geometry at all. Any specific degree figure you find online is coming from a retailer or a blog rather than from a veterinary body, and we are not repeating one. The practical substitute is to buy a product that publishes its run and its reach, work out the gradient yourself, and watch your own cat on it.

**Q: Is a ramp better than steps for an arthritic cat?**
A: Usually, on the mechanics. A ramp is one continuous surface, so the cat walks up it and never produces a vertical lift. Steps break one big jump into several smaller ones, which helps, but does not eliminate the movement that hurts. That is why both ramps rank above all five step sets on this page. Steps win on floor space, on ease of introduction, and on price, and for a cat who is hesitating rather than failing they are often the more realistic purchase. A household with room for a ramp at the bed and steps elsewhere has the best of both.

**Q: My cat will not go near the ramp I bought. What now?**
A: Give it two weeks rather than two days, and make the approach worth something. Treats at the base, then partway up, then at the top, repeated in short sessions, works better than any amount of lifting the cat onto it. Leave it in position permanently so it stops being an event. If she tries it once and backs off immediately, look at the surface rather than the slope — a tread the claws cannot grip reads as dangerous to an animal who already does not trust her own footing. And if nothing works, a shorter unit at a lower destination is a legitimate retreat.

**Q: What weight capacity does a cat ramp actually need?**
A: Far less than any product here provides, which is exactly why the number is still worth reading. The heaviest domestic cats sit around twenty pounds; the published capacities on this page run from 25 pounds to 150. No cat is going to stress any of them. What a published capacity tells you is that somebody specified and rated the product rather than sewing a cover over a block of foam, and three of these seven listings publish no capacity at all. Treat the number as a transparency signal rather than a strength requirement.

**Q: Should I be doing anything besides buying a ramp?**
A: Yes, and the ramp is the smallest part of it. VCA lists environmental changes alongside veterinary care, and the ones relevant to this page are providing non-skid floor surfaces and soft, padded bedding. None of that is treatment. Feline osteoarthritis is managed with a plan a veterinarian builds — weight, pain control, monitoring — and access equipment removes one daily insult rather than addressing the disease. Buy the ramp, and book the appointment, in that order of urgency reversed.
