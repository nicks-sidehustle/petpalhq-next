A carpet extractor is a machine that lays down water and cleaning solution, agitates it into the carpet with a powered brush, and then pulls it back out again. That last step is the entire point, and it is the step a mop, a rag, or a bottle of spray cannot do at all. For pet owners the distinction matters more than it does for anyone else, because the problem with urine is not on the surface where you can see it.

The IICRC — the body that certifies professional carpet cleaners — puts the mechanism plainly in its consumer guidance. Writing about musty and returning odors, it notes that in many cases the source comes from the backing of the carpet or other subfloor surfaces, which cannot be removed by cleaning the surface fibers of the carpet. It adds that homeowners often do not realise absorbent carpet padding can stay wet for an extended amount of time even when the surface fibers feel dry. Then comes the sentence every pet owner has lived: if the area becomes damp again or there is high humidity in the air, you may notice the odor returns. The IICRC's own quotation of the ANSI/IICRC S100 standard says the same thing from the other direction — elevated heat and humidity created from the cleaning process can amplify odors, so they are more evident during and after cleaning and during warm, humid conditions.

That is why this guide is about recovery, not about scrubbing. And it is why we have drawn a hard line around what belongs on this page.

## What this guide covers, and what it does not

**This guide covers full-size upright extractors only** — machines you push across a room, with a powered brush and separate clean and dirty tanks. **It deliberately excludes the compact spot-cleaner tier**: the portable handheld and canister machines built for one accident in one place. That is not an arbitrary split. Bissell draws the same line in its own buying guide, describing portables as machines to clean accidental spills and stains quickly from anywhere — including stairs, upholstery, and your car — while stating they are not ideal for cleaning wall-to-wall carpets.

The spot tier has its own home on this site. Our [How to Get Rid of Dog & Cat Urine Smell Permanently](/guides/how-to-get-rid-of-dog-cat-urine-smell-permanently-2026) guide is the removal protocol: finding every deposit under a UV light, the saturate-and-dwell enzyme method, and the compact extractor that belongs to that workflow. If your problem is one spot, start there and do not buy anything on this page. If your problem is a room — or a rental deposit, or a house you are about to sell, or a dog who was sick on the landing and the stairs and the hallway — you are in the right place.

## The heat question, which most buying guides get wrong

Here is the piece of advice that turns up in every pet-cleaning article, and it is worth understanding precisely because it is half-right. Humane World for Animals states it directly: avoid using steam cleaners to clean urine odors from carpet or upholstery, because the heat will permanently set the stain and the odor by bonding the protein into any man-made fibers.

That warning is real, and it is about steam cleaners. **None of the five machines in this guide is a steam cleaner, and none of them heats its own water.** Bissell's BigGreen Commercial specification for the BG10 lists "Heater: No" outright. Bissell states that the ProHeat's HeatWave Technology helps maintain the water temperature throughout the cleaning process by using the motor's heat, and that it does not heat the water itself. Hoover's HeatForce applies hot air to the floor to speed drying, not heat to the water in the tank.

The practical consequence is a one-line rule that no product page will give you: **on a urine spot, fill the tank with cool or lukewarm water, not hot.** These machines will happily deliver whatever temperature you pour in, and the temperature is your decision, not the machine's. That single choice is the difference between the Humane World warning applying to you and not applying to you.

One honest complication, because pretending it away would be dishonest: the Asthma and Allergy Foundation of America gives the opposite-sounding advice for a different problem, recommending that if you are going to use carpet, select one with a low pile and steam clean it often, and that wall-to-wall carpet be removed or steam cleaned in a home someone is allergic to pets in. These two authorities are not contradicting each other so much as optimising for different outcomes — Humane World is trying to stop a stain setting and a cat re-marking the spot; the AAFA is trying to lower the allergen load in the room. If you have both problems, treat the urine deposits cool first, and save any heat for general allergen cleaning on carpet that has never been soiled.

## The rinse pass almost nobody does

The other thing worth learning before you buy is that the last pass should be plain water. The IICRC repeats the instruction throughout its consumer guidance: if you own a home spotting or carpet cleaning machine, rinse the area with clear water only after no evidence of the stain remains.

The reason is soil, not chemistry. Under its Rapid Re-soiling heading, the IICRC lists detergent or soap residues not effectively rinsed during the cleaning process first among the causes, and adds that excessive soap residue left behind from untrained cleaners or DIY homeowner attempts is another. Elsewhere it explains the mechanism: products containing surfactants or detergents will attract soil to the area if not thoroughly rinsed, and over time new soil will stick to the residue, creating a dark area. There is also a warranty-grade version of the same warning — some disinfectants and oxy products may cause discoloration of textile furnishings and carpet if not fully rinsed and neutralized.

So the sequence that actually works is four steps, and only two of them involve the machine: blot and extract what is fresh, apply an enzymatic cleaner and let it dwell, extract the cleaner with the machine, then run one more pass with clear water only. The ASPCA names the chemistry for step two — clean accidents thoroughly with an enzymatic cleanser designed to neutralize pet odors — and adds a warning worth taping to the bottle: do not clean up accidents with an ammonia-based cleanser, because urine contains ammonia and cleaning with ammonia could attract your cat to the same spot to urinate again.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Will a carpet extractor remove urine that has soaked into the carpet pad?**
A: No, and no manufacturer in this guide claims it will. The IICRC is explicit that odor sources in the backing or subfloor cannot be removed by cleaning the surface fibers of the carpet, and Humane World for Animals states that where urine has soaked into the padding you may need to replace portions of carpet and padding. An extractor pulls moisture and solution out of the carpet and can reach the base of the yarns and the backing surface. It does not reach through to a saturated pad. If a spot has been soaked repeatedly over months, budget for pad replacement rather than a better machine.

**Q: Is a carpet extractor the same thing as a steam cleaner?**
A: No. An extractor sprays liquid, agitates it with a brush, and vacuums it back up. A steam cleaner uses heat to generate steam. None of the machines in this guide heats its own water — Bissell publishes "Heater: No" for the BG10 and states that the ProHeat's HeatWave maintains water temperature using the motor's heat rather than heating the water itself. That distinction matters on urine, because Humane World for Animals warns that heat will permanently set the stain and odor by bonding the protein into man-made fibers. Fill these machines with cool or lukewarm water on a urine spot.

**Q: Why does the smell come back a week after I clean the carpet?**
A: Because something was left behind. The IICRC gives two mechanisms. The first is moisture and depth — the source may sit in the backing or subfloor, and if the area becomes damp again or humidity rises, the odor returns, which the IICRC notes can happen even after professional cleaning when moisture reactivates it. The second is wicking, which the IICRC defines as the upward or outward migration of water-soluble materials on carpet fiber surfaces during drying: soil left at the base of the yarns and on the backing moves back to the yarn tips, where it becomes a visible stain again after the carpet dries. Both point the same way — extract more thoroughly, and dry faster.

**Q: Do I still need an enzyme cleaner if I own an extractor?**
A: Yes. They do different jobs, and the machine does not replace the chemistry. The ASPCA recommends cleaning accidents thoroughly with an enzymatic cleanser designed to neutralize pet odors, and VCA Animal Hospitals recommends an enzymatic cleaner to remove residual odors that could encourage a cat to return to previously soiled areas. The machine's job is to work that cleaner into the fibers and then get it — and everything it lifted — back out. The IICRC frames extraction the same way, describing a wet vacuum as the tool used to extract spotting and rinsing agents.

**Q: How much difference does tank size actually make?**
A: More than brush specifications do, on a whole-room job. Bissell's own buying guide notes that a ProHeat 2X Revolution Pet Pro can typically handle a 10-by-12-foot room before the dirty water tank needs emptying, while a smaller TurboClean PowerBrush covers about 8 by 8 feet per fill. Scaling that up is the real argument for the 1.75-gallon Big Green tanks over the 1-gallon machines: fewer interruptions, and less temptation to stop before the rinse pass.

**Q: Which machine should I buy if I have a puppy and a staircase?**
A: The ProHeat 2X Revolution Pet Pro Plus at $237.97. At 17.5 pounds it is the only pick here you can carry up a flight of stairs without planning the trip, it ships with an 8-foot hose and a pet upholstery tool for the stair treads themselves, and Bissell publishes a 5-year limited warranty on it. You will refill a 1-gallon tank more often than a Big Green's 1.75 gallons, which is the trade you are making.

**Q: Does the wider cleaning path on the cheaper machines mean they clean better?**
A: Not on its own. A wider path covers ground faster, which is why the 11.25-inch Hoover and the 11-inch ProHeat are the quicker machines per pass. Cleaning effectiveness on urine depends much more on how much liquid comes back up, which is where the 12-amp Big Greens and the two-motor BG10 — with its published 103-inch water lift and 2-gallon recovery tank — are working from different specifications entirely. Path width is a speed number. Water lift and recovery volume are the cleaning numbers.

**Q: Is buying a machine cheaper than renting one?**
A: It depends entirely on how often the accidents happen. A single rental is far cheaper than any pick here. Humane World for Animals suggests renting a carpet cleaner from a local hardware or grocery store as a reasonable option. The purchase case only builds if you are cleaning repeatedly — a dog with a medical issue, a cat in the middle of a litter-box problem, a puppy in month three of house-training — where the rental trips accumulate and the machine is available at 11pm on the night it happens.
