A travel setup is an order of operations, not a shopping list. Most households buy the crate first, because a crate is the biggest and most expensive object in the category, and then improvise the restraint. The evidence points the other way.

There is no federal safety standard for pet travel gear in the United States. The Center for Pet Safety, the nonprofit that fills that gap, put it plainly in its 2015 crate and carrier study announcement: "While many crate and carrier manufacturers claim their products are crash-tested and safe for use in a vehicle, there are currently no test protocols or performance standards in the U.S. to substantiate these claims." Its earlier pilot study is blunter still — "There simply are no existing standards in place."

That single fact reorders the whole purchase, because it means "crash tested" on a box carries no fixed meaning. What follows is sorted by how much checkable evidence stands behind each item.

## The Evidence Ladder

Three tiers exist in this category, and marketing works hard to blur them. Sorting your shortlist by tier is the most useful thing you can do before spending anything.

- **Third-party certified.** The Center for Pet Safety tested the specific product and published a record with a rating, a test date, and a protocol number. On this page: the Sleepypod Mobile Pet Bed (5 star, August 2024) and the Sleepypod Air (4 star, January 2025).
- **Documented manufacturer testing.** The company published a methodology you can read — the lab, the standard, the limits. Kurgo does this, naming Calspan in Buffalo, New York and Federal Motor Vehicle Safety Standard No. 213 for Child Restraint Systems. Weaker than certification, far stronger than nothing, and checkable.
- **Unspecified or absent testing.** A "tough tests" claim with no standard, lab, or figure — Dakota 283's position — or an explicit statement that the product was not tested, which is Rock Creek's position on its collapsible crate. Some of these are superbly built objects. None of them has checkable crash evidence.

The rule that falls out: the safety purchase is a restraint. The crate is a containment and durability purchase. A $690 crate does not buy what an $87 documented harness buys.

## The Crate Problem, Stated Exactly

It would be easy and wrong to say crates cannot be crash certified. They can, and several are. The Center for Pet Safety's published register includes Gunner G1 kennels in three sizes, Lucky Duck Lucky Kennels in three sizes, Cabela's GunDog kennels, and Rock Creek Crates' medium aluminium stationary kennel.

The problem is narrower and more practical: at our August 21, 2026 Amazon Creators API check, the certified crates we could identify were not buyable on Amazon. PetPalHQ only covers what a reader can actually buy on Amazon, so the two crates on this page are the buyable options — and neither carries a certification.

That leads to the most important sentence on this page. **A certification belongs to a product, not to a brand.** Rock Creek holds a genuine five-star CPS certification on its medium stationary kennel, and states on its own site that the collapsible "has not been crash tested and, by nature of design, does not incorporate the same internal welded structure as the Stationary crates." Both facts are true. A shopper who sees "Rock Creek" and "CPS five stars" in the same search result and buys the collapsible has bought a good crate and not the certified one.

The same rule applies on the certified side of the page: CPS lists the Sleepypod Mobile Pet Bed and the Mobile Pet Bed Mini as separate entries. Buy against the entry that matches your animal.

## Why Size Decides Your Shortlist

Both CPS-certified products on this page are cat-and-small-dog items. That is not a defect in the certification programme — crash testing is expensive and is performed on specific products at specific sizes — but it has a blunt consequence.

If your animal is small, buy at the top of the ladder. If your dog is medium or large, the certified tier on Amazon is simply not available to you, and the real choice is between documented manufacturer testing and nothing at all. Choosing Kurgo at that point is not settling; it is taking the strongest checkable evidence that exists at your dog's size.

The failure mode to avoid is buying a certified product and using it outside the size it was certified at, on the theory that certification is a general endorsement. It is a result, at a size, on a date.

## The Tether Problem

The cheapest thing in this category is the thing to avoid most firmly: the seat-belt-clip-plus-leash combinations sold as dog car restraints.

The mechanism is easy to picture. A tether gives the dog slack, slack lets the dog accelerate before the strap catches, and a body already in motion loads the hardware far harder than a body held in place. Every restraint on this page instead routes the vehicle's own seat belt through the harness or the carrier, which is why a correct fit feels uncomfortably snug the first time you install it. That snugness is the product working.

## The Rules Change Independently of Your Gear

Every product decision above sits underneath a layer of rules that no purchase satisfies on your behalf, and each moves on its own schedule. All of the following were read on August 21, 2026.

- **In the cabin.** The FAA's guidance states that it "allows each airline to decide if they will allow you to travel with your pet in the passenger cabin," and that where an airline does allow it, the container is carry-on baggage that must fit under the seat without blocking anyone's path to the aisle, be stowed before the last passenger door closes, and stay stowed while the aircraft is moving. The page carries its own stamp of December 4, 2025.
- **At the checkpoint.** The TSA's guidance is short: "Small pets are allowed through the checkpoint. Please check with your airline for their policy," and you remove the pet from the case, send the case through the X-ray, keep the pet under control on a leash, and remove the leash through the metal detector. Note that this particular page carries a stamp of May 5, 2017 — confirm current procedure with your airport if it matters to your itinerary.
- **Between states.** USDA APHIS states that it "doesn't regulate the interstate movement of pets by their owners" and that "Domestic movement requirements are set by the receiving State or Territory," which may include a health certificate, vaccinations, testing, or treatments. That page was last modified June 10, 2026.
- **Into the country.** Since August 1, 2024, the CDC's requirement for dogs that have been only in rabies-free or low-risk countries in the previous six months is the free CDC Dog Import Form, plus a dog that appears healthy on arrival, is at least six months old, and carries a microchip readable by a universal scanner. Dogs that have been in high-risk countries carry additional documentation.
- **In the car itself.** The AVMA's client brochure is direct on two points that no gear replaces: "Never leave pets unattended in a car," and "Consult your veterinarian before giving your pet any tranquilizers or sedatives. These can increase the risk of heart or respiratory problems and generally are not allowed by airlines." The ASPCA's guidance adds that leaving an animal in a parked vehicle "is illegal in several states."

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Is there a government safety standard for dog car restraints?**

A: No. The Center for Pet Safety's 2015 crate and carrier study announcement states that "there are currently no test protocols or performance standards in the U.S. to substantiate" manufacturers' crash-test claims, and its earlier pilot study says "There simply are no existing standards in place." That is why manufacturers who test at all borrow FMVSS 213, the federal child-restraint standard, and why a "crash tested" claim with no standard or lab named behind it cannot be checked.

**Q: Is a travel crate safer than a harness in a crash?**

A: For the crates on this page, that question has no evidence behind it either way. Rock Creek states its collapsible crate has not been crash tested, and Dakota 283 publishes no standard, laboratory, or measured figure. Certified crates do exist — CPS lists Gunner, Lucky Duck, Cabela's, and Rock Creek's stationary medium — but at our August 21, 2026 check we could not buy those on Amazon. If crash evidence is what you are buying, buy a certified or documented restraint and put it on the seat.

**Q: My dog is 70 pounds. What should I buy?**

A: A Kurgo harness, because there is no CPS-certified option on this page at that size. Kurgo's Impact product page states it was crash tested with simulated dogs up to 108 pounds and passed at Calspan in 2020; the Tru-Fit is named in Kurgo's methodology with published excursion limits up to 75 pounds. Kurgo does not appear on the CPS certified register, so this is documented manufacturer testing rather than third-party certification — the strongest evidence available in that weight range, and worth understanding for what it is.

**Q: Rock Creek has a five-star CPS certification. Doesn't that cover the collapsible crate?**

A: No, and Rock Creek says so itself. Its crash-testing page states that the collapsible crate has not been crash tested and does not incorporate the same internal welded structure as the stationary crates, and the CPS register lists the certified Rock Creek product as the Aluminum Kennel Medium — the stationary model. Certifications attach to specific products at specific sizes, never to a brand.

**Q: Does an "airline approved" carrier guarantee I can fly with my dog?**

A: No, and no authority grants that label. The FAA's own guidance says it allows each airline to decide whether pets may travel in the cabin, and that an accepted container counts as carry-on baggage subject to the usual stowage rules. A well-designed carrier is built to fit the common under-seat envelope; the specific airline and aircraft on your booking make the decision. Read the airline's published pet policy before you buy anything.

**Q: What paperwork do I need to bring a dog back into the United States?**

A: Since August 1, 2024, for a dog that has been only in rabies-free or low-risk countries in the previous six months, the CDC requires the CDC Dog Import Form — which is free, and whose receipt is valid for six months — plus a dog that appears healthy on arrival, is at least six months old, and has a microchip readable by a universal scanner. Dogs that have been in high-risk countries for dog rabies carry additional requirements, so check the CDC's own page against your specific itinerary.
