## Electric or Self-Warming

The two kinds of bed work differently. An electric bed has a heater. On every K&H bed here, K&H says the heater is thermostatically controlled to warm to your pet's body temperature when the pet is using it. The Thermo-Kitty Bed's page gives figures: about 15°F above room air when empty, and about 102°F once a pet lies on it.

A self-warming bed has no power at all. PangoVet explains: "Self-warming beds are plush beds that have an inside layer that is designed to reflect your cat’s body heat." That makes them safer around chewers, but they only return the pet's own warmth. K&H, which makes both kinds, says: "While our self-warming products are great for the indoors, they are not designed to return enough body heat for the cold outdoor temperatures of winter."

## The Pet Must Be Able to Step Off the Heat

Every vet source in this guide makes the same point. PetMD quotes Dr. Jo Ann Morrison, DVM: "Your pet needs to be able to quickly and easily move away from the heat if it becomes too warm." She adds: "Never allow them to lie directly on top of a supplemental heat source." The AKC says "It’s important to supervise your dog any time they’re using a heated dog bed or pet heating pad." Its warning about mobility is direct: a dog that can't get up easily is at risk of overheating or burns.

So put a heated bed in an open spot, never in a closed crate or carrier, and leave room around it. K&H's Thermo-Kitty Bed page says the same: "Always allow your pet enough space to be able choose if they want to rest on the pad or off the pad." For a dog whose main problem is stiff, sore joints rather than cold, the [orthopedic dog beds guide](/guides/best-orthopedic-dog-beds-senior-pets-2026) covers supportive foam beds without heat.

## Cords and Chewers

The cord is the risk that matters most indoors. PangoVet warns: "If your cat chews the electric cord or the pad, burns or electrocution might result." The AKC advises: "When buying a heated dog bed, look for a chew-resistant cord and keep the cord out of your dog’s reach to avoid injury." Of the electric picks, only the Lectro-Soft lists a steel-wrapped cord, and K&H still says it is not recommended for destructive pets.

For a chewer, both sources point the same way. The AKC says "a safer and low-tech alternative to electric beds is wireless or self-warming beds", and PangoVet says "Stick with a self-warming bed if your cat chews on cords or anything similar." The ThermaNAP and Mora Pets beds are this guide's no-cord picks. For a dog that destroys beds of any kind, the [chew-proof dog beds guide](/guides/best-chew-proof-dog-beds-2026) covers tougher unheated beds.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Are heated pet beds safe to leave plugged in?**
A: K&H says its heated beds are designed for 24/7 operation and can be left plugged in all day, and that MET Safety Laboratories suggests unplugging them if you leave home on an extended vacation. The AKC still advises supervising your pet any time it uses a heated bed or pad. Keep the cord out of reach, don't use an extension cord, and make sure the pet can get off the bed.

**Q: Why doesn't my heated cat bed feel warm?**
A: That is how the K&H beds are designed. K&H says the Thermo-Kitty Bed's surface stays about 15°F above room air until a pet lies on it, then warms to about 102°F. It also says its beds and pads are not pressure sensitive: they warm up because the pet's body insulates the pad.

**Q: Can I use my own heating pad or electric blanket for my dog?**
A: The AKC says no. About sharing a human heating pad or electric blanket with your dog, it says "this isn’t a good idea." Use a bed or pad made for pets.

**Q: Is a self-warming bed enough for winter?**
A: Indoors, it can be. A self-warming bed reflects the pet's own body heat and needs no outlet. Outdoors, it is not: K&H says its self-warming products "are not designed to return enough body heat for the cold outdoor temperatures of winter." For a pet that sleeps outside, see the outdoor cat house and dog house guides linked above.

**Q: Which pets benefit most from a heated bed?**
A: The AKC names senior dogs, short-coated breeds and dogs with certain medical conditions. PangoVet names hairless and short or thin-haired cats, plus cats that are underweight, ill, senior or kittens. For a sick, recovering or very young pet, K&H's own product page says to consult your veterinarian before use.
